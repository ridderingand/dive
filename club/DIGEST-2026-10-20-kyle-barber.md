## Summary

Ridd and his Inflight co-founder Kyle Barber (designer at Tinder, Facebook and Airchat per the YouTube description) tell the raw behind-the-scenes story of designing Inflight — a co-navigated episode rather than a guest interview. Arc: Kyle's origin at Airchat (the only designer, chasing feedback from Naval Ravikant and Brian Norgard over text and Signal; a side project to present work and collect feedback; a tweet) → the marketplace era (paste a Figma link + voiceover, someone sends $20, reviewers at ~$300/hr; economics don't work) → the "video cursor" moment ("I would use this instead of Loom") and Figma-plugin era → two designers with ~5% overlap (Ridd's Figma rigor vs Kyle's product-first instincts; the pool text that handed Ridd the design) → future archetypes (Kyle on product strategy; Ridd into engineering and now data/insight pipelines) → design thinking applied to company-building → positioning when "the pockets open and close very quickly" (betting on Figma, the voice-agent detour, chasing feature parity) → ICP "designers" not nearly narrow enough → fundraising as storytelling (the SPC pitch) → the breakthrough: delete every workflow except code prototypes; timing as the variable that matters most → the low points (responsibility to teammates, Cursor shipping the same thing, opportunity cost, the abandoned Mac app) → faith (the Figma Academy yellow-journal story) and trust → what's next (go-to-market with Tommy, community, MCP-first, a ChatGPT app in review).

Core claims: timing matters more than team, market or product, and Inflight was "a giant case study" — they needed the designer's way of working to cross into code first; a startup's designer is really doing positioning, and positioning in AI tooling shifts every few days; "designers" is not a narrow ICP — the fix was committing only to teams working in code and deleting Figma-, Lovable- and v0-only paths; going wide is tempting when AI accelerates building, but every surface becomes a failure point; the designer-founder role is a never-ending storytelling exercise where "the UX is serving of the storytelling and not the show"; the designer role has spread into product strategy, engineering and data ("a freaking product builder"); trust — including trust that a teammate will delete their own call (Soleio's "making sandcastles") — is the key to early-stage work. Why it matters: Ridd's own company story, first-hand, on going from Figma-era feedback tooling to code-prototype review.

**Granola summary Takeaways (generated, expanded)**

- Timing the market

Timing matters more than product, team, or execution. Kyle and Ridd kept building the right things at the wrong time, and it cost them.

Direct quotes:

- "The entire argument of the article was that actually timing matters more than literally every other thing when it comes to startups."
- "I think we might have just been a giant case study in the correctness of that because we needed the way of working. We needed the very nature of what it means to be a designer to kind of cross this chasm into this post-AI world."
- "A year ago, I don't think you could have told me that designers wouldn't be in Figma anymore."
- "The pockets open and close very quickly. I think that's been probably the hardest part of doing this in the midst of all the AI craziness."
- "We built too far in the future. We were just early."
- "I look at what I was doing a year ago and it's completely different than what I'm doing now."
- "Would sprinting and being decisive a year ago have taken us anywhere near where we are right now? Probably not."

Supporting ideas:

- They spent months on a voice agent concept. ListenLabs later sold for $2 billion doing something similar.
- Cursor launched a competing feature right as they were about to ship.
- Figma released a local make feature that killed one of their Mac app directions.
- The Figma plugin took 2 weeks to build then. Now it's one prompt.

---

- How the last year has changed our thinking on the role of designer

The designer role has exploded outward. Kyle and Ridd both moved far beyond UI into product strategy, engineering, and data, and they see that as the future archetype.

Direct quotes:

- "If you just liked building things, if you like creating things for someone, which I always thought was like my favorite part of design, it's just the craziest time."
- "I hope that it's not just completely stuck on, wow, I was only responsible for the UI of it. I think design thinking is such a powerful trait."
- "I feel like you've embraced a lot of the product strategy, higher level vision casting. And on the flip side, as somebody who's really motivated by craft, I like being in the details. The Venn diagram for me has been going really far into engineering."
- "AI is allowing me to get into the data too. You can really quickly whip up a PR to track everything you want to track, build pipelines to learn what you want."
- "That's a freaking product builder, I guess."
- "I'm glad we don't have to stay in that very small box that we were put in originally."

Supporting ideas:

- Ridd now owns significant amounts of code and is building his own insight pipelines with AI.
- Kyle has spiked hard into product strategy and vision, something traditionally outside the designer's lane.
- Both came from traditional product design roles but now have almost no overlap in day-to-day responsibilities.
- Kyle's dad was a carpenter. The furniture was built to be used, not looked at. That philosophy carried into how he thinks about design.

- The danger of building for too wide an ICP

"Designers" felt narrow enough as an ICP. It wasn't even close. Trying to serve every type of designer across every workflow created failure points everywhere.

Direct quotes:

- "I would have assumed at the beginning of doing this by saying, hey, our ICP is designers. That's narrow enough. Great. Turns out it's not even close to narrow enough."
- "We woke up one day where we're trying to make a product where the Figma plugin's broken. And we're like, damn, we haven't even used the Figma plugin in like 3 weeks."
- "We made this decision recently to basically just delete every other way of working. If you're only working in Lovable, sorry. If you're only working in Figma, sorry."
- "Especially when AI does accelerate some of the development, it's easy to tempt yourself into doing too much and going too wide and trying to be this all-in-one solution."
- "That's for sure one of the lessons. You got to get hyper narrow."

Supporting ideas:

- They tried to support Figma plugin users, Lovable users, v0 users, Power BI users, and preview deployment links all at once.
- A billion-dollar enterprise company asked for Figma-style comments. A different company needed something completely different.
- The fix was to commit only to teams working in code and delete support for everything else.

- The co-founder dynamic between two designers

Working with another designer for the first time revealed how different two people with the same job title can actually be.

Direct quotes:

- "This was the very first time that I've worked early stage with another designer in my entire career."
- "We're both technically like, I was an employee designer. We both had full product designer jobs, but I think we have like 5% overlap in the Venn diagram of what it means to be a designer."
- "I've used auto layout like one time in my life."
- "I remember when you first quote unquote onboarded me. You showed me like a real product. Like, you had made a real product, you designed all of it, it looked good."
- "You're so good at this. Like, you're just naturally really skilled at like the design system of it."
- "I'm a bully in Figma for sure."

Supporting ideas:

- Kyle came from Photoshop, thinks in folders, prioritizes getting things in front of users fast.
- Ridd came from a more systems-oriented, craft-focused background with deep Figma fluency.
- They eventually divided clearly: Kyle owns product strategy and vision, Ridd owns craft and engineering.
- The handoff happened organically after Kyle texted Ridd from a friend's pool saying he should take over the design.

- The low points of building a startup as a designer-founder

The hardest moments weren't about the product. They were about responsibility to the people who bet on you.

Direct quotes:

- "You are getting a bunch of people to bet on something that is paying them a lot less than a bigger company, spending a lot of their time."
- "We have incredible teammates. Like, they could be working at Frontier Labs right now or on way bigger things. Like, am I confident that people are going to make money here, that their time is worth it?"
- "The opportunity cost is so massive, both financially but also time. I work after my family goes to sleep and before they wake up most days."
- "I remember there's this moment where it just felt like there wasn't any more software companies to build."
- "I remember getting ready to launch and looking at it and being like, I don't want to work on this. Like, I'm not proud of this."
- "What am I doing? All those questions are in the brain."

Supporting ideas:

- Kyle has a second child on the way. The personal stakes are real. _[Granola misattribution: on the episode Kyle says it of Ridd — "you have a kid, you have another kid on the way" (00:36:00)]_
- Ridd watched peers from the same startup cohort get acquisition announcements and sign-on bonuses. He didn't want that, but it still created doubt.
- They built a robust Mac app in about a month and a half, then scrapped it because neither of them wanted to put their name on it.
- Cursor launched a nearly identical feature right before one of their planned launches.

- Urgency as the thing they wish they'd had earlier _[recording-only: none of this section's quotes are in the Descript composition — not asserted]_

They had funding, users, and feedback. What they lacked for too long was a sense of urgency to force conviction and ship.

Direct quotes:

- "If I could change one thing about how we did this, it was that we weren't urgent enough and we didn't feel enough pressure to maximize our own time and opportunity cost."
- "I can build a feature today. I can get feedback on it today. I need to make a call on like, is this good or bad? And I can make that today."
- "We need to stop wasting time here, essentially."
- "Once we started doing that, I think we all became like, okay, cool, this feels right."
- "I haven't looked back from probably 2 months ago maybe when that was fully felt real for me."
- "We kind of hid behind the mask of being able to internally test this as much as we wanted with as many teams as we wanted before we really said this is what InFlight is."

Supporting ideas:

- They had all the tools to move fast. The bottleneck was mental, not technical.
- _[omitted: recording-only fundraising/investor detail, not in the published episode]_

- Storytelling as the core skill of a designer-founder

Positioning, fundraising, and product direction all come down to the same thing: convincing yourself something is true and then communicating it clearly.

Direct quotes:

- "The whole thing is just this never-ending storytelling exercise. Like when you're both a designer and founder, you realize that actually everything is just storytelling and the UX is serving of the storytelling, not the show."
- "To be good at that, you have to convince yourself something's true, right? And as designers, like a lot of time we're meant to be open-minded and be like, 2 things can be true. And like, put your foot down, be like, this is correct. And what a hard thing to do as a designer."
- "People looking to deploy capital are trying to get you to echo one of their own convictions, essentially, like how they see the world."
- "You have to pull on something that is unique to you versus the other 100 people that are pitching them similar ideas."
- "I never felt like fully confident that we had that. And so maybe that's why we always held off a little bit."

- _[omitted: recording-only fundraising/investor detail, not in the published episode]_

- Faith as a decision-making framework

Ridd makes major life and career decisions based on what he feels God is leading him toward, and he has a track record of it working out in surprising ways.

Direct quotes:

- "I think people would probably almost get uncomfortable if they realize the degree to which I make really big decisions solely off of what I feel like God is leading me in."
- "I think it's the only reason I'm still doing InFlight. I'll put it that way."
- "I have this track record where whenever I feel like I hear from God and then make a decision, I immediately get offered like a really shiny opportunity immediately. Like I have like 5 of these in a row."
- "I wrote it in yellow and I wrote it and it was basically, it just said like, say no to those 2 opportunities and this other path will actually financially be better."
