## Summary

Geoff Teehan (co-founder of Teehan+Lax; ex-Facebook/Meta; now design lead at Lightspark — "VP of Design" on dive.club, "chief design officer" in Ridd's spoken intro) walks Ridd through how a weekend hackathon game (Dialed) became a viral side business, his career arc from 90s rave flyers to global payments infrastructure, and how he thinks about design when building becomes nearly free. Why it matters: a veteran's clean statement of "abundance → ownership/permanence" and a concrete small-team operating model.

- Why Geoff only wants to hire 4 designers

  Geoff runs a five-person design team (himself plus four designers) and believes that is the ceiling, even as the company grows. The reasoning is not just headcount efficiency but a specific philosophy about team shape.

  Direct quotes:
  - "I think at our company size and even as big as we want to get, I don't think I need more than what I have today, which is myself and four designers. I really don't."
  - "I think that the level of horsepower that we have, not just because of the people, but because of the tools and the people and our culture and our product, I feel like that is ample."
  - "What's the superpower that you bring that none of my other team has? Like, how do you level this up? Because if I hire all the same archetype, like we all care about the same things, that's great, but like no one's going to really improve as much as maybe you want."
  - "They have a baseline of greatness, and then they've got a superpower. And I think that that has been a huge part of it."

  Supporting ideas:
  - First hire was Jay: well-rounded, strong visual designer, good product thinker, no handholding required
  - Each subsequent hire brings something unique the others don't have
  - Without distinct superpowers, gaps emerge (e.g. motion graphics) and you end up outsourcing to people who don't share your quality bar
  - AI tools are a multiplier that makes a small team viable at a scale that previously required more people
- His photography analogy with digital cameras and photographing parking spots

  Geoff's most memorable framework from the episode. He argues that we are still in the early phase of AI-powered software, where people are using the new tool the same way they used the old one, and the real explosion of abundance hasn't happened yet.

  Direct quotes:
  - "With photography, the cost of film made you think before you press the shutter, right? You had 24 shots or whatever you had in your film, you're being really mindful of the things that you were shooting."
  - "When digital cameras showed up, people used them like film cameras. Same pictures, right? No one was photographing their parking spot yet with their digital camera."
  - "Just faster development, the same pictures without the development costs, and our parking spot phase hasn't started yet."
  - "That's where I think the what deserves to last comes. And like, what do you commit to? Because building is going to become free."
  - "I need this, and now I have working software."
  - "95% of it is going to be shitty. And shitty software is easy to deal with. It just goes away."

  Supporting ideas:
  - The parking spot phase = the moment when people stop using AI to do the same things faster and start doing entirely new categories of things
  - When that happens, the key question becomes: what deserves to be made permanent? Who owns it? Who commits to it?
  - Geoff draws a direct line from this to the http://rose.gg story: turning an internal tool into a real product required auth, deletion flows, sign-up, marketing site, and a real commitment to users
- The http://dialed.gg origin story and going viral

  Geoff built the first version of http://dialed.gg in roughly 2 days as a hackathon project, posted it on Threads, and watched it explode to 5,000 simultaneous players.

  Direct quotes:
  - "I'm going to make this dumb game where I'm going to show you a color for a few seconds, and then I'm going to change the color and give you HSB controllers to try and recreate it."
  - "In 48 hours, I had multiplayer."
  - "At one point it was like 5,000 people were playing it simultaneously. And I was like, oh my God, this is incredible."
  - "It was 8,000 lines of code, which was cool because I could ship it instantly. Any change was just like, there's no build process, just ship."
  - "I caught the moving bus and I have a real, I have a day job that requires all of my attention."

  Supporting ideas:
  - Inspired by a college color theory professor who said humans are terrible at remembering colors
  - Built as a single HTML file, hiding and showing DOM elements
  - Zero friction was the core design principle: no app download, no sign-up, just enter the URL and play
  - That zero friction drove virality but killed retention
  - His son Cole quit his job at a bank in Toronto to run it full time
  - Now 7 games, iOS app in review, display ads, auth, and a backend hire from Upwork for big releases
- How Geoff thinks about zero friction and what it costs

  Direct quotes:
  - "Don't download an app, don't have to sign up. You just, you enter the URL and you immediately start playing."
  - "That sucks for retention, but it is awesome for like when something goes viral because it makes it absolutely frictionless."
  - "Retaining those people becomes the issue, which is why we saw such a rise and such a fall."

  Supporting ideas:
  - The tradeoff is explicit and intentional: optimize for virality at the cost of retention
  - This is a consumer design principle applied to a web game, and it worked exactly as predicted
  - The same principle shows up in http://rose.gg: turning it into a product required adding all the friction (sign-up, deletion, marketing) that the internal tool didn't need
- How Geoff runs feedback on his design team

  Geoff runs a daily 9:15am standup built around reviewing work in progress, not status updates. The culture is blunt, fast, and collaborative.

  Direct quotes:
  - "Our tendency here is to share it really early and to share it really often."
  - "It's no holds barred. It's all very, very respectful."
  - "If you think that this just looks like shit, just tell me it looks like shit. And maybe you have some ideas, maybe you don't. It's okay if you don't have ideas."
  - "Take the Figma file and just go riff, or we'll all riff on it in the meeting. Everybody will just have their laptops and be like, hey guys, let's just spend 15 minutes and help try and figure out what the thing is here."
  - "We're in the work, not just talking about it."
  - "I don't do one-to-ones. We're not having a bunch of career talks. You're going to know when things aren't going well."

  Supporting ideas:
  - Lightning rounds: if someone is stuck, they call a 4pm session, all designers spend an hour riffing, problem solved almost always
  - No ego is the non-negotiable: people who pocket-veto feedback or won't show work until it's perfect are disqualifying
  - You can step on anybody's toes and no one cares, because the shared goal is the work being as good as possible
  - Geoff is willing to speak first in this environment, which he would not do in a larger crit setting
- How Geoff brings consumer design principles into B2B and enterprise work

  Direct quotes:
  - "I mean, it's all the same. I think having worked at an industry where you touch so many different verticals and so many different types of work, you can be surprised at what you'll find interesting as a designer."
  - "Some of the more perceived boring things are just so critical, and so useful and so impactful."
  - "These are still human beings that are ultimately using the system. And if it's designed really well, they'll use it more. They'll use it more efficiently. They'll think more positively about it. They'll trust it more, and they'll become your advocate."
  - "Just because it's global payments infrastructure doesn't mean you can't give it some life."

  Supporting ideas:
  - Lightspark's marketing site has sound design on hover states: the consumer DNA is visible even there
  - The team mocks up full native app prototypes for enterprise pitch clients, so they can tap through and understand how a payout system would work inside their own app
  - Geoff's most surprising example: redesigning a mutual fund site for financial advisors in the mid-2000s turned out to be more satisfying than Red Bull or Nike work, because the problem was real and tangible
- The copying problem and what it means for designers who ship original work

  Direct quotes:
  - "I didn't have an iOS app, still don't. It started with just a ton of copycat websites who were like, using our name, they basically just like told whatever agent to copy the game."
  - "If you would release dialed today, I'm sure I could point Fable 5.1 at it and spit out something that looks really freaking similar in like what, 90 minutes?"
  - "You might hit on something really big and then 24 hours, somebody can take it, make it better, and become the winner. That's gonna happen a ton."
  - "There's also the visual identity, like the stylistic taste, this like expression that comes out of us that now you put on Twitter, you see it, you take a picture and you feed it into Claude."
  - "The cost of copying is free."

  Supporting ideas:
  - Geoff used "trap streets" (a cartography technique) in his original iPhone PSD kit to catch people who stole and redistributed it
  - That trick doesn't work for software: there's no equivalent fingerprint you can embed
  - He still thinks a hat tip or credit is the right move for small borrowings, but a full idea copy is a different thing entirely
  - He started using em dashes again in his writing as a signal that a human wrote it
- What Geoff thinks happens to design managers who aren't close to the craft

  Direct quotes:
  - "I fear for like the managers who are not deep in IC work, or like capable of good IC work."
  - "I think it's going to be managers who build just like ICs, and they just have an extra role on top that just gets layered on top."
  - "I do wonder about what happens with those folks that just have been out of the design game for a long time and aren't experimenting or close to the metal when it comes to development."

  Supporting ideas:
  - Large companies will still need structure and leadership, but middle management that doesn't produce is at risk
  - The value of a manager who is great at managing people but not at making things is shrinking as team sizes shrink
  - Geoff's own career is the counter-example: he has reinvented himself multiple times and is still shipping as an IC while leading a team
