## Summary

Danny Martinez (CEO and founder of Decimals — the platform Ridd uses to run the Dive Talent Network) gives a state-of-the-market read on design hiring for both designers and hiring managers. **Granola summary Takeaways (generated, expanded)**

- The discrepancy between what "design engineer" means and general thoughts on how titles are evolving
  - Hiring managers use the same title to describe fundamentally different people: "Do you want a designer who knows how to use code? Because there's plenty of those. Or do you want somebody who trained as an engineer, has a background as an engineer, that can design themselves?"
  - Oscar Lozano rolled his eyes at the term: "I just feel like a lot of people call themselves that when in reality, they're not."
  - The word "engineer" is losing meaning broadly. Recruiters are now "talent engineers." A new role called "launch engineer" appeared this week.
  - Danny's simplified framing: two very different profiles, same label. One is a designer who codes, the other is an engineer who can design.
  - Matt Silva just says "I craft products. Design and engineering meet." Simple, effective, no inflated title.
  - Preston Booth calls himself "product designer and builder." Not elegant, but it works.
  - Danny floated "product design engineer" as a way to proactively combat the buckets people put you in when you say "design engineer."
  - Oscar's day-to-day is "night and day" compared to 18 months ago. The silos of engineering and design, the handoff points, "all of that's ripped out."
- Green and red flags from hiring managers
  - Biggest red flag: big company logos. "People are almost allergic to the big company logos."
  - Ex-FAANG experience used to carry weight. Now hiring managers ask: "Have they really been at the forefront? Have they really been tinkering?"
  - The Microsoft example: a designer with 9 years at Microsoft, high-profile roles. "The first few times that I shared his portfolio, people didn't even want to chat."
  - He rebuilt his portfolio around the last 18 months of building and tinkering. "All of a sudden, everybody wants to talk to this guy. And all it was was a repositioning exercise."
  - Biggest green flag: showing you've been cooking. "The number one signal right now" is proving you've been building over the last 12 to 18 months.
  - AI-generated or templated portfolios are becoming a red flag. One hiring manager said: "I've seen this one before." Lack of originality reads as thoughtlessness.
  - Siddharth's framing: "It's almost like I want to show you the proof of human."
  - Writing is a major green flag. "If you can put your thinking into writing and I can get through it and not think that it was written by Claude, that is" a strong signal.
  - Pasting AI-generated content is "an anti signal." Thoughtful, personal learnings are a green flag.
- What we can learn from the portfolios of people who are getting hired in the talent network
  - Preston Booth (https://prestonb.xyz): mid-career designer from BambooHR. Built a full universe for his portfolio. Command K, hover states, Rive animations, a personal video, little games. "You can look past his experience." Now at Clerk.
  - Key lesson from Preston: "Your company doesn't need to be at the forefront. You just need to be playing in your spare time."
  - Claire Taylor (https://claire.io): simple and elegant on the surface, but the details reveal craft. Hover states, a reel-style work presentation, and a Substack with her writing.
  - The single interaction that stood out to a hiring manager on Claire's site: hover on a project, cursor changes, little peek of content appears on the left. "I actually don't think I've seen this on a portfolio before."
  - "One good creative, well-executed interaction can go so far right now."
  - Claire actively turns down companies that only want to see the final artifact: "It's not interesting for them to just see the final artifact."
  - Matt Silva (https://mattsilva.dev): more engineering background, no traditional resume structure. Leads with "I craft products. Design and engineering meet." Dumps you into UI labs and side projects. Now a design engineer at Polymarket.
  - Matt's inspiration came from a German mechanical camera brand. "That's the feeling that I want to go for." The portfolio feels analog, like switching a dial.
  - Ini Abiodun (https://iniabiodun.com): plays with all the senses. Music, lighting, an "atelier" feel. Lawyer by background. "I must feel like I'm in a member's club now."
  - Common thread across all four: "They built a little universe that you step into when you open up their website."
  - Hiring managers are clicking through 50 links in an hour. Standing out emotionally matters.
  - Avoid Figma links or Dribbble as a portfolio. "You don't get a second chance to create a first impression."
- Advice for hiring managers wanting to attract top talent
  - The best designers are already employed. "A lot of cases, these people are already in jobs. So you really need to come in and compete."
  - In-demand designers are often juggling 5 to 6 interviews simultaneously.
  - Hannah Ann (Head of Design at Superpower) sets the bar. When she saw a portfolio she loved: "I would kill a thousand gorillas to be able to hire this designer."
  - Danny screenshotted that message and sent it to the candidate. The candidate said: "I want to frame this. This is so cool. I feel so special."
  - Personalized outreach matters. Decimal built a feature for hiring managers to send personalized notes. One hiring manager went further and recorded a 3-minute video walking through the candidate's portfolio before the first interview.
  - New startups assume every designer wants to work for them. "That is just not the case."
  - One in-demand designer Danny knows didn't respond to any company that didn't lead with access to an executive or head of design in the first touchpoint.
  - Matt Herzog at Life Kit leads with a 1:1 conversation himself, not a recruiter screen. "You are putting somebody who is not a designer to do a screen for a design job. That doesn't make any sense."
  - Long processes are a red flag. "There is nothing valuable that you get from giving me eight, nine, forty-five minute interviews with people that are all asking me different variations of the same question."
  - Hannah Ann's approach: quick vibe check, then straight into a paid work trial. "If the vibe is good, and some basics are checked, get straight into the work."
  - Focus on the back-and-forth during the work trial, not just the artifact. "I want to see how they react the first time that I tell them that I disagree with something."
  - Old norms like ghosting candidates will cost you. "The best ones are in demand."
- The state of comp for designers right now
  - One person has more leverage than ever before. That doesn't mean teams hire fewer people, but the people they do hire command higher salaries.
  - Design engineers and senior product designers in SF and New York are making $300k or close to it. "I would have expected that to be engineering salaries."
  - Supply and demand: "If there's not enough supply, then you're going to need to pay and compete more."
  - Brand design is the hardest role to hire for right now. Companies think they can pay brand designers less than product designers or design engineers. Brand designers are pushing back.
  - One brand designer Danny knows is freelancing at $500k to $750k per year. His baseline for a full-time conversation.
  - Brand designers with systems thinking are realizing they can build a system, hand it off, and not need to be full-time. "Maybe you hire me as a freelance. Maybe hire me as a contractor."
- Danny's founder journey and the lessons from building Decimal
  - Started Decimal about a year ago with no recruiting background. Background as a designer only.
  - Contrarian early bet: "If we manage to create an experience that candidates really love, we'll be able to get the best candidates, and hence the companies will follow."
  - Most founders in the space focus on the company side because that's where the money comes from. Decimal went the other direction.
  - What surprised him most: what hiring managers say and what they do are completely different. A hiring manager sets hard rules on salary, location, and office days. Then the dream candidate shows up and "all of those rules go out the window."
  - "Those two artifacts," the resume and the job description, "are really, really not worth the paper that they're written on."
  - Beginner's mindset is the most important mindset right now given the rate of change.
